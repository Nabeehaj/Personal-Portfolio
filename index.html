About

I've always had a question.

Usually followed by another one.

Growing up, I was the kid who asked why about absolutely everything.

Why does this happen? How does that work? But what if we did it differently?

My family probably deserves an award for the number of questions they've had to answer. And, to be fair, not all of them were particularly important.

Some weren't even remotely relevant.

I wasn't being nosy. I was just curious. (About the wrong things sometimes, but that's beside the point.)

The funny thing is, I never really grew out of it.

I still ask too many questions. I still find myself going down rabbit holes over the smallest details. And I still have a hard time accepting "that's just how it works" as an answer.

Somewhere along the way, that curiosity found its way into computer science.

Now, here's where I'd love to tell you I was a child prodigy who started coding at five, mastered twenty programming languages by twelve, and built my first app before learning long division.

Unfortunately, that's not my story.

I wasn't born knowing how to code. I didn't have some extraordinary head start. And most of what I know today didn't come easily.

It came from trying, failing, asking more questions (shocking, I know), and trying again.

I've always had to work for the things I wanted to be good at. And somewhere along the way, I learned to appreciate that process just as much as the result.

I might not know every programming language or have an answer to every technical question. But the things I do know? I've put in the work to understand them.

Not just how to make something work, but why it works in the first place.

Because I've never been interested in collecting skills just to say I have them. I'd rather understand something deeply than pretend to know everything.

And that's what keeps me coming back to computer science.

There's always something new to figure out, something I haven't learned yet, or a problem that makes me question everything I thought I understood.

Sometimes it's frustrating. Sometimes it's humbling. Sometimes it's a missing semicolon.

But every time I finally figure something out, I'm reminded why I started.

Not because I was born knowing the answers.

But because I've always been willing to look for them.

And if there's one thing that hasn't changed since I was that little kid driving my family crazy with questions, it's this:

I've never been particularly good at leaving questions unanswered.

And I don't plan on starting now.
